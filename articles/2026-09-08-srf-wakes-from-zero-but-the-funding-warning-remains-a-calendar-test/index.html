SRF wakes from zero, but the funding warning remains a calendar test

The Standing Repo Facility accepted $0.02B on September 8: unusual against a near-zero base, not a large flow. Calendar-driven reserve pressure is rising, but SOFR below IORB still points to abundant overnight cash.

The Standing Repo Facility accepted $0.02B on September 8. It is the fresh board change: a wake-from-zero observation with a 13.49 level reading in Seiche’s monitor. It is not a large use of the facility.

The narrower conclusion is that dollar-funding capacity faces a dated test, not a demonstrated break in the price of cash. Reserves have declined, Treasury settlements are approaching, and Seiche’s reserve-path measure places the system below its estimated reserve-demand kink. But SOFR remains below IORB, while the SRF backstop is not being used in size.

Seiche’s composite reads 44.7, EROSION. Its largest contribution comes from the dated reserve path: the weather component is saturated at 100.0 and contributes 11.0 points. That is a structural warning, not an observed market price.

The mechanism or historical setup

The mechanism concerns the timing and location of deployable cash, not simply the aggregate quantity of reserves. Reserves fell $30.4B in the week through September 2 to $2,895B. The ledger attributes $17.2B to a rebuilt Treasury General Account, $14.5B to the residual, and $6.3B to a larger Federal Reserve balance sheet. Six legs sum to the reserve change; $37.5B, or 0.56% of assets, remains in the unnamed residual.

Treasury settlement can concentrate cash demand among institutions that intermediate secured funding. Seiche identifies $246B of settlement on September 10 and $119B on September 15 while reserves sit below the estimated kink. The chain is observable: settlement absorbs deployable balances; dealers and lenders become more selective in allocating balance sheet; then the price of secured overnight funding rises relative to IORB if the constraint binds.

The desk guide’s comparison with the September 2019 repo spike is context, not a forecast. Its limited lesson is that corporate tax payments and Treasury settlement needs can coincide with less immediately deployable cash than calm aggregate indicators imply. A large reserve total can coexist with a local shortage of usable intermediation capacity.

Quantified evidence with as-of context

The balance-sheet evidence is dated but concrete. Reserves were $2,894.531B as of September 2. Seiche’s fitted kink was $3,703.4B. The fit’s R-squared was 0.617, making the kink a structured estimate rather than a precise threshold.

The official-sector footprint moved in the same direction through September 2. Foreign officials reduced Federal Reserve custody Treasuries by $45.7B over 13 weeks while foreign reverse-repo parking rose $33.5B. The combined footprint nevertheless fell $12.2B to $2,958B; Seiche calculates that 27% of the custody decline left the official sector rather than rotating within it.

The calendar supplies the immediate forcing. For September 10, Seiche projects reserves at $2,927.5B, with a $2,874.8B worst case around the $246B settlement. For September 15, the corresponding figures are $2,907.3B and $2,836.9B around the $119B settlement. September 16 is another calendar-pressure date, with a $2,809.9B worst case. By September 30, the forecast is $2,883.7B and the worst case is $2,749.8B.

The divergence is real but incomplete: Seiche’s plumbing percentile is 65.0 while the market percentile is 24.0, a 41-point lead for plumbing over price. The pooled five-business-day event read is 6.2%. A separate machine-learning output does not beat climatology on probability levels and is published for ranking and alerting, not as literal odds.

The strongest counter-case

The counter-case can defeat the thesis because it rests on the current price of cash. SOFR was 0.8 basis points below IORB as of September 2, an abundance signal rather than a scarcity print. A reserve estimate below a fitted kink does not override that observed relationship.

Nor does the SRF reading establish stress. The $0.02B acceptance is statistically unusual because the base was near zero. It is not evidence that the Federal Reserve backstop is being tested in meaningful size.

Broader pricing offers little corroboration. Market stress stood at the 24th percentile of its own history as of September 4. The VIX was 15.3 as of September 7 and high-yield option-adjusted spreads were 2.65% as of September 3. If secured funding remains cheap relative to IORB and broader stress stays subdued through settlement, the structural warning will have failed to become a funding event.

The published September 30 forecast also argues against declaring a fracture now. It is a 5.2-basis-point move, within a 1.3-to-9.9-basis-point band, with severity rated 2 on a scale from 1 calm to 5 extreme. The published forecast is labelled naive, not modelled.

A falsifiable next test

September 10 is the first observable test. The calendar-pressure thesis gains support only if stress migrates from the reserve path into plumbing: SOFR ceases trading below IORB or shows upward pressure, and SRF use rises above $0.02B.

The thesis weakens if the September 10 and September 15 settlements, and the September 16 pressure date, pass with SOFR still below IORB, SRF use remaining near $0.02B, and reserves nearer Seiche’s forecast path than its adverse cases. That would show that reserve distribution or private intermediation absorbed the calendar demand more easily than the structural indicators suggested.

September 30 is the broader test. A move within the published 1.3-to-9.9-basis-point band would be consistent with manageable seasonal pressure rather than a system-wide funding break.

Follow the pressure chain

Follow the sequence rather than collapsing distinct risks into one claim: reserve drains and Treasury settlement; lenders’ and dealers’ willingness to deploy balance sheet; then the observable price of secured overnight dollars. The first link is visible. The second is plausible but not directly established by the evidence here. The third has not broken.
